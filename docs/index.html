Where is water hardest to reach in Sindh?

A drinking-water access score for 5,159 villages, built only from open satellite and geospatial data. The usual way of building such an index turns the ranking upside down, making Pakistan's driest district look well served.

Key findings

Annual rain hides the dry months

Most published water indices treat annual rainfall as supply. In Sindh the monsoon falls in a few weeks of July and August and is gone by March, so annual totals point the wrong way.

71.5→45.9

Mithi (Tharparkar), Pakistan's driest district: near the top with annual rainfall, last of 25 with dry-season rainfall.

Last in every one of 500 weight bootstraps
14.5%

Average share of a village's annual rain that falls between November and May. The range runs from 0.6% to 39.6%.

Annual vs dry-season indicators: r = −0.67
88.6%

Villages whose nearest perennial water is a canal, not a natural river. Natural hydrography alone misses nine villages in ten.

4,573 canals vs 586 rivers
ρ +0.72

Districts scored most accessible are among the least safe. Thatta scores 72.9, yet every published water sample there was unsafe.

6 districts · PCRWR 2021; Solangi et al. 2017

Accessibility is not potability. The index has no salinity data, so the coastal delta, where seawater has spoiled groundwater, scores well.

Method

Five open indicators, one score

Each indicator is log-transformed where skewed, winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles, and rescaled to 0–100 so that higher always means better access.

Rin S · 45%

Distance to perennial water

HydroRIVERS (discharge over 1 m³/s) merged with OpenStreetMap canals.

closer = better
Win S · 45%

Dry-season rainfall

CHIRPS daily rainfall, November–May, averaged over 2020–2024.

more = better
N20%

Surface water

Sentinel-2 NDWI, 2023 cloud-masked median, mean within 1 km.

more = better
P20%

Population within 2 km

WorldPop 2024, constrained and UN-adjusted, 100 m grid.

fewer = better
E15%

Elevation

SRTM 1 arc-second. Lower ground sits nearer the water table.

lower = better
S     = max(R, W)
Score = 0.45·S + 0.20·P
      + 0.20·N + 0.15·E   // 0–100

Rivers or canals and rainfall are substitutes, not complements. A canal past the village makes rain matter less; in the Thar desert, with no canal, rain is everything. Taking the better of the two avoids penalising either kind of village. Bands are percentiles: Poor is the bottom 20%, Good the top 20%.

Answered

Accessible

Is water likely within reach?

A relative score per village. District rankings hold across weighting choices (Spearman ρ 0.84–0.96).

Not answered

Safe to drink

Is the water potable?

There is no salinity data. Thatta and Badin rank near the top, yet that is where seawater intrusion has made groundwater undrinkable.

Partly

Checked on the ground

Does the score match reality?

21 groundwater samples near Thatta all exceed 500 mg/L TDS beside villages rated Good. The checks show the access–safety gap, not score accuracy.

  • Scores are relative. 70 means better served than most Sindhi villages, not adequate by any external standard.
  • The population indicator barely moves the score (correlation 0.06), and its direction, more people meaning worse access, may be wrong.
  • Rainfall is coarse. CHIRPS cells are about 5.5 km and undercount dry-season rain in hyper-arid areas, so Thar's figures are lower bounds.
  • River discharge is naturalised. HydroRIVERS models flow before abstraction, so the lower Indus carries less than it shows.

SWAI asks whether open satellite and geospatial data can show village-level water access where household surveys stop at the district. Its main lesson is methodological: the indicator choice that looks standard reverses the answer.
